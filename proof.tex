{\small\sffamily\bfseries\color{teal}RECTANGLES, SQUARES, AND INTEGER DESCENT}\par
\vspace{6pt}
{\LARGE\sffamily\bfseries\color{ink}A geometric interpretation of Vieta jumping}\par
\vspace{3pt}
{\large\sffamily\color{muted}IMO 1988 Problem 6}\par

{\sffamily Pranay Manocha\hfill September 2026}\par

\textbf{The jump is a leftover width.}
Placing $k$ copies of an $a\times b$ rectangle alongside one another and removing
a $b$-square produces the width $ka-b$. The construction gives an area interpretation
of the classical Vieta descent. This note makes the integer and termination
arguments explicit; no claim of priority is made.

\stephead{1}{The problem and the strip}
\textbf{Problem.} Let $a,b$ be positive integers and suppose that
$k=(a^2+b^2)/(ab+1)$ is an integer. Prove that $k$ is a perfect square~\cite{imo}.

Order the lengths so that $1\le a\le b$. Then $k\ge1$ and
\[
 a^2+b^2=kab+k.
\]
The two squares have the same total area as $k$ copies of an $a\times b$
rectangle and $k$ unit squares. The rectangles, each of width $a$ and height $b$,
form a strip of width $ka$. Define $c=ka-b$.

\textbf{Why the square fits.} We must first show $c\ge0$. If $c<0$, integrality
gives $b-ka\ge1$, so
\[
 k=a^2+b(b-ka)\ge a^2+b>b.
\]
But $b\ge ka+1\ge k+1$, a contradiction. Thus $c$ is a nonnegative integer.

\diagram{construction}{Figure 1. The leftover width is the Vieta jump. The displayed example has $a=8$, $b=30$, $k=4$.}

Removing the $b\times b$ square leaves a $b\times c$ rectangle. Its area is
\[
 bc=kab-b^2=a^2-k,\qquad\boxed{a^2=bc+k.}
\]
This is an equality of areas. It does not assert that the long leftover
rectangle fits inside the small square without being cut.

\newpage
\stephead{2}{The next smaller pair}
The strip gives $b+c=ka$ and $bc=a^2-k$. Consequently
\begin{align*}
 a^2+c^2 &=bc+k+c^2\\
 &=c(b+c)+k\\
 &=kac+k=k(ac+1).
\end{align*}
Hence the pair $(c,a)$ satisfies the same equation, with the same $k$.
In area terms, the new squares match $k$ new $c\times a$ rectangles together
with the same $k$ units of area.

Moreover,
\[
 0\le c=\frac{a^2-k}{b}<\frac{a^2}{b}\le a.
\]
Thus the move
\[
 \boxed{(a,b)\longmapsto(ka-b,a)=(c,a)}
\]
strictly decreases the sum of the lengths. When $c>0$, the new pair is ordered,
positive, and integral, so the same construction applies again.

\diagram{descent-1}{Figure 2. A first descent with $k=4$: $(30,112)\mapsto(8,30)$.}

\stephead{3}{Why the descent terminates}
There cannot be an infinite strictly decreasing sequence of positive integer
sums. Every pair with two positive lengths admits the next move, so eventually
one length becomes zero. Write the final pair as $(0,x)$, where $x>0$ is an integer.
The preserved equation is then
\[
 0^2+x^2=k(0\cdot x+1),\qquad\boxed{k=x^2.}
\]
At the final construction the strip and the large square coincide. The leftover
rectangle has zero width, and the other square has area $k$. This proves the
claim. The case $k=1$ is included: $(1,1)\mapsto(0,1)$. \hfill$\square$

\textbf{A stronger conclusion.} Since
$\gcd(ka-b,a)=\gcd(a,b)$, each move preserves the greatest common divisor.
At the endpoint $\gcd(0,x)=x$. Therefore $k=\gcd(a,b)^2$, a strengthening also
given in Campbell's 1988 treatment~\cite{campbell}.

\newpage
\stephead{4}{Four rectangles at every step}
For $(a,b)=(30,112)$, the quotient is $13444/3361=4$. The complete descent is
\[
 (30,112)\longmapsto(8,30)\longmapsto(2,8)\longmapsto(0,2).
\]
The area identities are $900=896+4$, then $64=60+4$, then $4=0+4$.
The integer $k$ stays fixed while the lengths shrink.

\diagram{descent-2}{Figure 3. The complete $k=4$ descent, shown as a symbolic chain.}
\diagram{terminal-case}{Figure 4. The final jump: $(2,8)\mapsto(0,2)$. The remaining square has area $4=2^2$.}

\textbf{Reading the diagrams.} Each strip is drawn in proportion to its labelled
dimensions. Separate constructions may have different scales to keep their
geometry legible. The four unit squares at the endpoint are literal unit cells;
earlier area comparisons do not specify a dissection of the smaller square.

\newpage
\stephead{5}{Relation to the standard proof}
Regarding the original equation as a quadratic in its second coordinate gives
\[
 t^2-kat+(a^2-k)=0.
\]
The roots are $b$ and $ka-b$. Exchanging them is precisely Vieta jumping.
The strip construction makes the second root visible as a remaining width.
The number-theoretic mechanism is the classical descent; this note develops
its repeated-rectangle interpretation.

\stephead{6}{Context and references}
The arithmetic descent is established prior work. Campbell's early treatment
includes the greatest-common-divisor conclusion~\cite{campbell}. Wu presents
a geometric visualization using integer points on a hyperbola, including the
$k=4$ numerical orbit used here~\cite{wu}. A 2016 online discussion examines
a remainder-style descent and includes a separate geometric attempt~\cite{moody};
that discussion is not a premise of the proof given in this note.

The rectangle strip provides an area interpretation of the same transformation.
These references establish context, not a comprehensive priority investigation.
No claim is made that this particular presentation is historically new.
